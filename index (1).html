AP Stats — formulas and variables

Your reference sheet. Start here and return to it any time you forget a formula.

What are you measuring?

Mean = average of numeric measurements (height, years, points, money).

Proportion = fraction/percent with a characteristic (on-time, female, satisfied).

Sample mean x-bar = average from ONE sample. Sample proportion p-hat = fraction from ONE sample.

Sampling distribution = distribution of a statistic over all possible samples of the same size.

Variables — what to plug in

SymbolMeaning and what goes in it
NPopulation size: total number of items in the entire group.
nNumber of observations in ONE sample, not the number of samples.
xIndividual numerical score; one measured value.
x̄ (x-bar)Sample average: add the sample values and divide by n.
μ (mu)Population mean: the average given for the whole group.
σ (sigma)Population standard deviation: the spread given for individual population values.
pPopulation proportion: the given population percent written as a decimal.
p̂ (p-hat)Sample proportion: number meeting the condition divided by n.
1, 2Subscripts identify the first and second groups. Keep the order consistent.
DFirst group's statistic minus second group's statistic.
SEStandard error: standard deviation of the sampling distribution.
zHow many standard errors the cutoff is above/below the center.
CutoffThe boundary asked about in the probability question. Not necessarily the mean.

One sample mean

Use when measuring one group's average.

Mean = μ
SE = σ/√n
z = (cutoff − μ)/SE

Shape: normal population OR sufficiently large n (n ≥ 30 is a common rule of thumb, not a guarantee for strongly skewed data).

One sample proportion

Use when measuring one group's fraction or percentage.

Mean = p
SE = √[p(1 − p)/n]
z = (cutoff − p)/SE

Shape: approximately normal if np ≥ 10 AND n(1 − p) ≥ 10.

Difference of two independent sample means

Use when comparing two groups' averages.

D = x̄₁ − x̄₂
Mean(D) = μ₁ − μ₂
SE(D) = √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂)
z = (cutoff − Mean(D))/SE(D)

Shape: both populations approximately normal or their samples sufficiently large (for each group, its population is approximately normal or its sample is sufficiently large).

Difference of two independent sample proportions

Use when comparing two groups' percentages.

D = p̂₁ − p̂₂
Mean(D) = p₁ − p₂
SE(D) = √[p₁(1 − p₁)/n₁ + p₂(1 − p₂)/n₂]
z = (cutoff − Mean(D))/SE(D)

Shape: n₁p₁, n₁(1 − p₁), n₂p₂, n₂(1 − p₂) must all be ≥ 10.

All standard error formulas assume independent observations/groups. For random samples without replacement, verify n is no more than 10% of the relevant population for the usual AP formula.

Combinations / all possible pairs

N choose 2 = N(N − 1)/2

Example from vehicles: 5 × 4 / 2 = 10. Add the two values, divide by 2, and repeat for every pair.

Probability directions

Greater / exceeds → RIGHT tail → 1 − CDF(cutoff).

Less / lower → LEFT tail → CDF(cutoff).

At least 10 percentage points lower → D ≤ −0.10 (if D = first − second).

Exceeds the other group → D > 0.

CDF returns the probability to the LEFT of the cutoff.

Desmos

normaldist(mean,SE).cdf(cutoff)

Left-tail probability.

1-normaldist(mean,SE).cdf(cutoff)

Right-tail probability.

normaldist(mean,SE).pdf(x)

Draws the normal curve, NOT a probability. Replace mean, SE, and cutoff with your numbers or define them first.

Do not accidentally use the population SD instead of the standard error. Subtract means, ADD variances, square root at the end.

How to choose a formula

Average, height, weight, score, dollars, years = sample mean (x-bar).

Percent or fraction of people/items meeting a condition = sample proportion (p-hat).

Two groups = difference (first group minus second group).

μ = population mean; σ = population standard deviation.

p = population proportion; n = number of observations in ONE sample.

SE = standard deviation of the sampling distribution (how much the statistic varies).

In Desmos, normaldist(mean, SE).cdf(cutoff) gives area to the LEFT. For area to the RIGHT, subtract that answer from 1.

Assume independent samples and the 10% condition where needed, as in the answer key.

Tap any card to see a tiny example of what it means.

Population = the whole group. Sample = a smaller group.

A parameter describes a population (μ, σ, p). A statistic is calculated from a sample (x̄, s, p̂). A bar or hat often tells you you're looking at sample data.

Read the little marks

What do the bar, hat and tiny number mean?

μ (mu) is an average for everyone. x̄ (x-bar) is an average from a sample. The bar tells us we averaged numbers. p is a population percentage; p̂ (p-hat) is a percentage from a sample. The hat tells us it is an estimate from data.

Subscripts 1 and 2 label the groups. They do not mean multiply by 1 or 2. In the SAT example, group 1 is math and group 2 is reading. Keep that order for the entire problem. A superscript ² means square; 107² = 107 × 107, not 107 × 2.

When a percentage is given, divide by 100 before entering it in a proportion formula: 84.1% = .841. A difference of .10 is 10 percentage points.

How do I know which number belongs to which symbol?

Circle the description beside each number in the question. Do not guess from the size of the number. A mean is an average, a standard deviation is a measure of spread, and a sample size is a count of selected observations.

μLook for “population mean,” “average,” or “mean of all …”.

σLook for “population standard deviation” or “standard deviation of the distribution of individual values.”

nLook for “a random sample of …” or “selects …” followed by a count.

pLook for a known population percent/proportion. Divide a percentage by 100.

x̄Average calculated from the sample; not necessarily the population mean.

p̂Percent calculated from the sample, written as a decimal.

μ for the sampling statisticThe expected value (center) of the graph you are about to use.

SEThe standard deviation of the sampling statistic; calculate it using the correct one- or two-group formula.

CutoffThe target number in the probability question, not necessarily the sample mean or population mean.

1 and 2Labels for the first and second groups, in the order the question compares them.

Example: “mean 527, standard deviation 107, a sample of 50” translates directly to μ = 527, σ = 107, n = 50. The words tell you where each number goes.

03

How to compare graphs

Look at three things: shape, center, and spread.

Shape = what the graph looks like. Center = where the values cluster. Spread = how far apart the values are. The horizontal axis tells you what is measured; the vertical axis tells you frequency, probability, or density.

Bias vs. variability

From the Estimator A and B question: both estimate a true population standard deviation of 1. The graphs below illustrate their different spreads.

Both have low bias because they center near the true value, 1. A has lower variability because its estimates are closer together.

Bias: Is the center in the right place?
Variability: How wide is the graph?

What happens when the sample gets bigger?

Each sample average becomes more consistent. Its standard error gets smaller. The Central Limit Theorem says the distribution of means tends toward normal as sample size increases.

SE = 6.32

This example assumes a normal population with μ = 50 and σ = 20. Move the slider and watch the curve become narrower; its center stays at 50.

If the original population is normal, the sample mean is normal even for small samples (assuming independence). If the population is skewed, n ≥ 30 is a common starting guideline; stronger skew may require more data.

How to compare two graphs in a question

1. Read the horizontal axis: are the numbers scores, sample means, sample proportions, or estimates? 2. Find the center: which graph is centered farther to the right? 3. Compare spread on the same scale: which covers a wider range? 4. Look at the tails: is either skewed?

Bias is different from variability. If the true value is 1 and both graphs center at 1, both are approximately unbiased. If A is narrower, A is more consistent. A narrow graph can still be biased if it is centered at the wrong number.

For the retail sales question, the sample size is 36 days, not 150. The 150 is the number of samples collected. The center stays $783, and the SE is 106/√36 = $17.67. A larger sample generally makes a sample-mean graph narrower and more normal.

All four formulas together:

Question typeCenter (μ)Standard error (SE)
One meanμσ / √n
One proportionp√[p(1 − p) / n]
Two meansμ₁ − μ₂√(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂)
Two proportionsp₁ − p₂√[p₁(1−p₁)/n₁ + p₂(1−p₂)/n₂]
Two groups: Subtract their centers, but add their variances inside the square root. The formulas shown assume independent samples; for samples without replacement, check the 10% condition (sample at most 10% of each population) when applying these standard errors.

Is the distribution approximately normal?

Means: The original populations are approximately normal or the sample sizes are sufficiently large.

Proportions: Check np ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10 for each group. This is the large-counts condition.

1 − p is the proportion that does not meet the condition. For example, if p = .80, then 1 − p = .20.

Why there are four different formulas

Means describe measured numbers, so they already have a population standard deviation (σ). Proportions describe yes/no results, so their spread comes from p(1 − p), the variability of yes/no outcomes. Two independent groups each contribute uncertainty, so their variances add even when we subtract their averages or proportions.

05

Turn the words into a probability

Once you know the center and standard error, find the cutoff and which side to shade.

z = (cutoff − center) / SE

For a sampling distribution, use the standard error, not the original population σ. A z-score tells you how many standard errors a cutoff is away from the center. It is not the probability itself.

Less than / below

Shade the area LEFT of the cutoff.

Greater than / exceeds

Shade the area RIGHT of the cutoff.

In Desmos, the CDF gives the area to the left. For a right-tail probability, subtract from 1.

normaldist(0,1).cdf(1)Example: less than z = 1 → about 0.8413.
1-normaldist(0,1).cdf(1)Example: greater than z = 1 → about 0.1587.
Comparing two groups? Keep the order the same throughout. If you calculate A − B, then “A exceeds B” means A − B > 0, and “A is at least 10 percentage points lower than B” means A − B ≤ −0.10.

Translate the words before touching the calculator

“Math exceeds reading” → Math − Reading > 0. If both averages are equal, the difference is zero. If math is higher, the difference is positive. Zero is the cutoff; −6 is the expected difference, not the cutoff.

“American is at least 10 percentage points lower than Spirit” → American − Spirit ≤ −.10. A sample with American .60 and Spirit .70 has a difference of −.10. A difference of −.15 is even lower, so it counts too. Shade LEFT.

Desmos gives the probability as a decimal. Multiply by 100 to turn it into a percent. A probability of .0069 is .69%, not 6.9%. A z-score is not a probability: z can be negative, but probabilities are always from 0 to 1.

SAT · Math vs. Reading and Writing

Two means

Math scores: mean 527, SD 107, sample of 50. Reading/writing scores: mean 533, SD 100, sample of 100. What's the probability that the sample average for math exceeds reading/writing?

VariableMath (1)Reading (2)
μ = population mean527533
σ = population SD107100
n = sample size50100

1. Choose the formula category and check the shape.

Clues: “sample mean SAT math score” and “sample mean ERW score.” These are two averages, not percentages.

Choose two sample means because you are comparing the averages of two independent samples. Both original populations are approximately normal, so the sampling distribution of the difference is approximately normal. Keep math as group 1 and reading/writing as group 2.

Statistic: x̄₁ − x̄₂

x̄₁Average SAT math score in the sample of 50.

x̄₂Average SAT reading/writing score in the separate sample of 100.

We will find the center with μ₁ − μ₂, the spread with the two-means SE formula, and then the chance that the difference is positive.

2. Find the center (mean of the difference).

Use this formula:

μ of (x̄₁ − x̄₂) = μ₁ − μ₂

Why this formula? The question compares two sample averages by subtracting math minus reading. The expected difference equals the difference of the two population averages. Use this whenever you need the center of a two-independent-means sampling distribution.

What goes into each symbol:

μ₁ = 527Population mean for SAT math. The question gives “mean of 527.”

μ₂ = 533Population mean for reading/writing. The question gives “mean of 533.”

Now substitute the numbers:

μ₁ − μ₂ = 527 − 533
= −6

What this means: The center of the graph is −6 points. Math is expected to average 6 points lower. This is the center, not the probability cutoff.

3. Find the spread (standard error).

Use this formula:

SE = √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂)

Why this formula? There are two independent sample averages. Both samples vary, so both contribute to the spread. For an individual sample mean, the variance is σ²/n; add the two variances and take the square root. Use this formula when asked for the standard deviation of a difference of two independent sample means.

What goes into each symbol:

σ₁ = 107Population SD for SAT math.

n₁ = 50Number of math scores selected.

σ₂ = 100Population SD for reading/writing.

n₂ = 100Number of reading/writing scores selected.

² and √Square each σ first; divide by its sample size; add; then take the square root.

Now substitute the numbers:

SE = √(107²/50 + 100²/100)
= √(11449/50 + 10000/100)
= √(228.98 + 100)
= √328.98 ≈ 18.14

What this means: The sampling distribution of differences has a standard deviation of about 18.14 points. Do not subtract the two variances.

4. Find the cutoff and the z-score.

Read the words: math “exceeds” reading means the math sample average is greater than the reading sample average.

P(x̄₁ − x̄₂ > 0)

If the two averages are equal, their difference is zero. If math is 550 and reading is 540, their difference is +10. We want any positive difference, so the cutoff is 0 and the graph is shaded to the right.

Use this formula:

z = (cutoff − center) / SE

Why this formula? Use a z-score to express the probability cutoff in standard errors from the center of this normal sampling distribution. You can also enter the original center and SE directly into Desmos without calculating z.

What goes into each symbol:

cutoff = 0The question asks math to exceed reading; a positive difference starts at zero.

center = −6The expected difference found in step 2.

SE ≈ 18.14The standard error found in step 3.

Now substitute the numbers:

z = [0 − (−6)] / 18.14
= 6 / 18.14 ≈ 0.331

What this means: Zero is 0.331 standard errors above the center. The z-score is not a probability.

5. Find the probability and shade the graph.

Use this formula:

P(D > 0) = 1 − CDF(0)

Why this formula? D is shorthand for x̄₁ − x̄₂. Desmos CDF gives the area to the left, but the question asks for an area to the right, so subtract from 1.

What goes into each symbol:

mean = −6First input to normaldist; from step 2.

SD ≈ 18.14Second input to normaldist; from step 3. Use the unrounded formula for accuracy.

cutoff = 0Input to .cdf(); from step 4.

What this means: The shaded right tail is the chance math beats reading. Copy the Desmos line below to calculate its area.

1-normaldist(-6,sqrt(107^2/50+100^2/100)).cdf(0)
Answer: 0.3704 = 37.04% chance.

Airlines · American vs. Spirit

Two proportions

American is on time 84.1% of the time, and Spirit 75.4%. We sample 65 American flights and 50 Spirit flights. What is the probability that American's sample proportion is at least 10 percentage points lower than Spirit's?

VariableAmerican (1)Spirit (2)
p = population proportion0.8410.754
n = sample size6550
1 − p = not on time0.1590.246

1. Choose the formula category and check the shape.

Clues: “on-time percentage” and “sample proportion.” Whether a flight is on time is a yes/no outcome. There are two airlines, so choose two sample proportions rather than means.

Statistic: p̂₁ − p̂₂

p̂₁Sample proportion of on-time American flights.

p̂₂Sample proportion of on-time Spirit flights.

Use this formula:

np ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10

Why this formula? For proportions, both the expected success and failure counts must be at least 10 in each group for the normal approximation.

What goes into each symbol:

p₁ = .84184.1% of American flights are on time.

n₁ = 6565 American flights sampled.

p₂ = .75475.4% of Spirit flights are on time.

n₂ = 5050 Spirit flights sampled.

Now substitute the numbers:

American: 65(.841) = 54.665
American: 65(1 − .841) = 10.335
Spirit: 50(.754) = 37.7
Spirit: 50(1 − .754) = 12.3

What this means: All four expected counts are at least 10. With independent samples (and the usual 10% condition for sampling without replacement), the sampling distribution is approximately normal.

2. Find the center (mean of the difference).

Use this formula:

μ of (p̂₁ − p̂₂) = p₁ − p₂

Why this formula? We compare two percentages by subtracting American minus Spirit. The center of this sampling distribution is the difference of their population proportions. Use this formula when asked for the expected difference between two sample proportions.

What goes into each symbol:

p₁ = .841American population proportion: 84.1 ÷ 100.

p₂ = .754Spirit population proportion: 75.4 ÷ 100.

Now substitute the numbers:

p₁ − p₂ = .841 − .754
= .087

What this means: Expect American to be 8.7 percentage points higher. This is the center of the graph, not the cutoff.

3. Find the spread (standard error).

Use this formula:

SE = √[p₁(1−p₁)/n₁ + p₂(1−p₂)/n₂]

Why this formula? Two independent proportions have two sources of variability. A proportion has variance p(1−p)/n, not σ²/n. Add both variances, then take the square root.

What goes into each symbol:

p₁ = .841On-time American population proportion.

1 − p₁ = .159Not on time: 1 − .841. All outcomes together have probability 1.

n₁ = 65Number of American flights sampled.

p₂ = .754On-time Spirit population proportion.

1 − p₂ = .246Not on time: 1 − .754.

n₂ = 50Number of Spirit flights sampled.

Now substitute the numbers:

SE = √[.841(1−.841)/65 + .754(1−.754)/50]
= √[(.841×.159)/65 + (.754×.246)/50]
≈ .07594

What this means: The spread is about .07594 in proportion units, or 7.594 percentage points. Use the population proportions, not the 10-point probability cutoff.

4. Find the cutoff and the z-score.

Read the words: American is “at least 10% lower” than Spirit. Here the difference is in percentage points. Example: .60 − .70 = −.10. A difference of −.15 is also at least 10 points lower.

P(p̂₁ − p̂₂ ≤ −.10)

Use this formula:

z = (cutoff − center) / SE

Why this formula? Use the sampling distribution center and standard error to turn the cutoff into a z-score.

What goes into each symbol:

cutoff = −.10Ten percentage points lower: 10 ÷ 100 = .10, with a minus sign.

center = .087American minus Spirit population proportions (step 2).

SE ≈ .07594The sampling standard error (step 3).

Now substitute the numbers:

z = (−.10 − .087)/.07594
= −.187/.07594 ≈ −2.46

What this means: The cutoff lies 2.46 standard errors below the center. Shade the left tail because we want differences at or below −.10.

5. Find the probability and shade the graph.

Use this formula:

P(D ≤ −.10) = CDF(−.10)

Why this formula? D stands for p̂₁ − p̂₂. The CDF already gives the left-tail area, so do not subtract from 1.

What goes into each symbol:

mean = .087Expected difference (step 2).

SD ≈ .07594Standard error (step 3); use the original expression for accuracy.

cutoff = −.10Probability boundary from step 4.

What this means: The tiny left-tail area is the probability American will be at least 10 percentage points below Spirit. Use the Desmos line below.

normaldist(.087,sqrt(.841*.159/65+.754*.246/50)).cdf(-.10)
Answer: 0.0069 = 0.69% chance.

One mean · Maple leaves

Average length above 4.8 inches

The population is approximately normal with mean 4.5 inches and SD 0.5 inches. We sample 10 leaves. Find the chance the sample average is greater than 4.8 inches.

1. Choose the formula and find the center.

Use this formula:

μ of x̄ = μ

Why this formula? We are averaging lengths from one group. For a single sample mean, the center equals the population average.

What goes into each symbol:

μ = 4.5Mean length of all maple leaves.

x̄Average of the 10 selected leaves.

Now substitute the numbers:

Center = 4.5 inches

What this means: The original population is approximately normal, so the sample mean is approximately normal even with n = 10.

2. Find the spread.

Use this formula:

SE = σ / √n

Why this formula? Averages of samples vary less than individual leaves. Use the one-mean formula rather than the proportion or two-group formula.

What goes into each symbol:

σ = .5Population standard deviation of leaf lengths.

n = 10Number of leaves in the sample.

Now substitute the numbers:

SE = .5 / √10 ≈ .1581

What this means: Average lengths have less spread than individual leaf lengths.

3. Find the probability.

Use this formula:

z = (cutoff − center) / SE

Why this formula? We need P(x̄ > 4.8), so this is a right-tail probability.

What goes into each symbol:

cutoff = 4.8Target average length.

center = 4.5Population mean.

SE ≈ .1581From the previous step.

Now substitute the numbers:

z = (4.8 − 4.5)/.1581 ≈ 1.897

What this means: Desmos: subtract its left-tail CDF from 1. Use the unrounded standard error.

1-normaldist(4.5,.5/sqrt(10)).cdf(4.8)
Answer: .0289 = 2.89%.

One proportion · Gallup

More than 85.3% satisfied

78% of people in the population are satisfied with their personal lives. A sample of 150 people is taken. Find the chance the sample proportion is greater than .853.

1. Choose the formula and find the center.

Use this formula:

μ of p̂ = p

Why this formula? Satisfied or not is a yes/no outcome. The question is about one percentage from one population, so choose one sample proportion.

What goes into each symbol:

p = .78The given population percentage, 78 ÷ 100.

n = 150Number of people surveyed.

p̂Sample proportion of satisfied people.

Now substitute the numbers:

Center = .78
np = 150(.78) = 117
n(1−p) = 150(.22) = 33

What this means: Both expected counts are at least 10, so the normal approximation is appropriate under the usual independence condition.

2. Find the spread.

Use this formula:

SE = √[p(1−p)/n]

Why this formula? This is the spread of one sample proportion. A yes/no percentage gets its variance from p(1−p).

What goes into each symbol:

p = .78Population proportion satisfied.

1 − p = .22Population proportion not satisfied.

n = 150Sample size.

Now substitute the numbers:

SE = √[.78(1−.78)/150]
= √(.78×.22/150) ≈ .03382

What this means: This is the spread of the sample proportions, not of individual people.

3. Find the probability.

Use this formula:

z = (cutoff − center) / SE

Why this formula? The question says “greater than .853,” so shade the right tail.

What goes into each symbol:

cutoff = .853The target sample proportion, 85.3%.

center = .78The expected sample proportion.

SE ≈ .03382From step 2.

Now substitute the numbers:

z = (.853 − .78)/.03382 ≈ 2.158

What this means: Use 1 − CDF in Desmos. Such a high sample percentage would be unusual if the population percentage were really 78%.

1-normaldist(.78,sqrt(.78*.22/150)).cdf(.853)
Answer: .0155 ≈ 1.55%.

Daily retail sales

Daily sales are skewed right: μ = $783, σ = $106. Each random sample contains 36 days; 150 sample means are collected.

1. Pick the formula and check shape

Use this formula:

μₓ̄ = μ; SE = σ/√n

Why this formula? This is one average. The 36 days in a single sample determine SE; 150 is the number of times the sampling is repeated.

What goes into each symbol?

μ = 783Population average daily sales.

σ = 106Population standard deviation.

n = 36Days per sample, not 150.

Now substitute:

μₓ̄ = 783; SE = 106/√36 = 106/6 = 17.67

By the usual CLT guideline, n = 36 supports approximate normality for this problem.

106/sqrt(36)
Answer D: approximately normal; mean $783; SD $17.67.

5.5–5.7 · Q3 · One mean

Homeowners

Ownership durations are approximately normal with μ = 12.9 years, σ = 4.1 years. Sample 9 homeowners; find the chance the sample average exceeds 14 years.

1. Calculate center and spread

Use this formula:

μₓ̄ = μ; SE = σ/√n

Why this formula? We have one group and an average. The population is approximately normal, so even n=9 supports the normal model.

What goes into each symbol?

μ = 12.9Population mean years.

σ = 4.1Population SD years.

n = 9Homeowners in the sample.

Now substitute:

μₓ̄ = 12.9; SE = 4.1/√9 = 1.3667

The sampling distribution is narrower than the original population.

2. Find the probability

Use this formula:

z = (cutoff − μ)/SE

Why this formula? Exceeds means greater than, so use the right tail.

What goes into each symbol?

cutoff = 14The boundary the question asks about.

μ = 12.9Center of the sample means.

SE = 1.3667Standard error from step 1.

Now substitute:

z = (14 − 12.9)/1.3667 ≈ 0.805

Use 1 − CDF to find the area to the right.

1-normaldist(12.9,4.1/sqrt(9)).cdf(14)
Answer B: approximately 0.2104, or 21.04%.

5.1–5.4 · Q5 · One proportion

Green sea turtles

77.5% of green sea turtles in the region are female. Sample 200 turtles. Find the probability that at least 75% are female.

1. Choose the formula and check shape

Use this formula:

SE = √[p(1 − p)/n]

Why this formula? One percentage, so use the one-proportion formula. The population proportion supplies the spread, not the cutoff.

What goes into each symbol?

p = .77577.5% population proportion.

1 − p = .225Complement: proportion not female.

n = 200Sample size.

cutoff = .75At least 75% in the sample.

Now substitute:

np = 155; n(1 − p) = 45; SE = √(.775 × .225/200) ≈ .02953

Both expected counts exceed 10; the sampling distribution is approximately normal.

2. Write the probability expression

Use this formula:

z = (cutoff − p)/SE

Why this formula? At least means greater than or equal to, so shade right.

What goes into each symbol?

cutoff = .75Boundary given in the question.

p = .775Center of the sampling distribution.

SE = .02953Calculated spread.

Now substitute:

z = (.75 − .775)/√(.775 × .225/200) ≈ −.847

The question asks for the correct expression, not necessarily a decimal probability.

1-normaldist(.775,sqrt(.775*.225/200)).cdf(.75)
Answer B; approximate probability 0.8014 (80.14%).

Four teachers

The original values are 2, 7, 12, 25. Select two without replacement.

1. Count and average all pairs

Use this formula:

N choose 2 = N(N − 1)/2; x̄ = (x₁ + x₂)/2

Why this formula? We need every distinct pair and its average.

What goes into each symbol?

N = 4Four original values.

n = 2Two values per sample.

Now substitute:

4 × 3/2 = 6 possible samples

Pairs: (2,7), (2,12), (2,25), (7,12), (7,25), (12,25). Means: 4.5, 7, 13.5, 9.5, 16, 18.5.

2. Identify the distribution and shape

Use this formula:

P(x̄ = value) = frequency / total

Why this formula? Each of the six distinct means occurs once. Each has the same probability.

What goes into each symbol?

frequency = 1Each mean appears once.

total = 6Six equally likely pairs.

Now substitute:

P = 1/6 for each mean

Sampling distribution of x̄; discrete uniform on these six possible means and symmetric around 11.5. Uniform refers to equal probabilities, not equal spacing.

dotplot([4.5,7,9.5,13.5,16,18.5])

Two means · Birth weights

Choosing a standard-error formula

Male babies: mean 3622 g, SD 432 g, sample of 6. Female babies: mean 3465 g, SD 414 g, sample of 8. The question asks only for the standard deviation of the difference of sample means.

1. Identify the formula and plug in values.

Use this formula:

SE = √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂)

Why this formula? Birth weights are numerical measurements. The question compares two independent sample averages and asks for their standard deviation, so use the two-means spread formula. No need to subtract their population means here.

What goes into each symbol:

σ₁ = 432Population SD of male birth weights.

n₁ = 6Number of male babies sampled.

σ₂ = 414Population SD of female birth weights.

n₂ = 8Number of female babies sampled.

Now substitute the numbers:

SE = √(432²/6 + 414²/8)
= √(31104 + 21424.5) ≈ 229.19 g

What this means: Answer C. Squaring and adding the independent variances gives the standard deviation after taking the square root.
